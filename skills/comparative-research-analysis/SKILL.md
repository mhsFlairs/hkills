# Comparative Research & Gap Analysis

Produce decision-useful comparisons without confusing marketing claims, internal status, and verified facts. This skill is about the research and analysis process; do not assume a particular company, product, or industry.

## Workflow

1. **Clarify the decision and scope.** Identify what is being compared, the intended audience, the decision the analysis should support, and requested deliverables. Ask one focused question when a missing choice materially changes the analysis. Otherwise state reasonable scope assumptions briefly and proceed.
2. **Collect evidence.** Use authoritative, current sources where available: official product documentation, release notes, customer/partner material, and user-provided internal context. Record source URLs and publication/access dates for researched claims. Prefer primary evidence; use secondary sources to corroborate or explain, not to invent implementation details.
3. **Label provenance.** Distinguish clearly among public-source evidence, user/team-provided status, analysis/inference, and unknowns. Internal statements supplied by the user are not independently verified unless they have been checked against an authorized source. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence; say “not reported” or “not confirmed” rather than asserting nonexistence.
4. **Normalize the comparison.** Map comparable capabilities and use consistent readiness labels suited to the evidence, such as live, building, researched/prototyped, planned, partial, or not reported. Define labels once. Do not imply equivalent implementation merely because two features share a name.
5. **Synthesize for a decision.** Separate where each side appears stronger, direct capability mappings, gaps to close, and strategic implications. For each actionable gap, explain the user need, proposed capability or workflow, prerequisites/dependencies, measurable evidence of success, and key risks. Prioritize by user value, competitive importance, readiness/reuse, effort, and uncertainty; explain the rationale rather than presenting an unexplained score.
6. **Handle timelines conservatively.** Use relative horizons unless authoritative dates are supplied. Label estimates as proposals, not commitments. State material assumptions and dependencies, and distinguish scope-completion estimates from production readiness. Never turn a user’s tentative timeframe into a promise.
7. **Write with calibrated claims.** Attribute vendor-reported outcomes to the vendor. Avoid absolutes (for example, “always,” “100%,” or “has no equivalent”) unless strong evidence supports them. Preserve uncertainty and competing evidence, especially for broad public claims.
8. **Deliver and check.** Match the requested format. Check that every major conclusion is traceable to evidence or explicitly marked as analysis; readiness and timing statements agree across sections; gaps lead to actionable recommendations; and the result answers the stated decision.

## Recommended structure

Adapt to the request; do not add sections merely to fill a template.

- Executive takeaway and scope/evidence note
- Where each side is ahead, with material caveats
- Capability/readiness mapping
- Prioritized gap analysis: what to fill, how, dependencies, and success measures
- Priority matrix and proposed catch-up sequence, if requested
- Sources, assumptions, and measurement guardrails

## Guardrails

- Do not research or expose non-public internal information without authorization.
- Do not fabricate source links, roadmap commitments, accuracy claims, or implementation details.
- Keep capability, maturity, deployment availability, and observed outcomes distinct.
- Treat proposed accuracy targets as benchmark goals requiring defined datasets, sample sizes, and measures—not guarantees.
- Respect the requested scope; do not drift into product design or feature implementation unless requested.
